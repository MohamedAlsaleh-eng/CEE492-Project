Figure - Selection of the ridge penalty for polynomial regression
File: ridge_tuning.png (vector version ridge_tuning.pdf is produced locally; *.pdf may be git-ignored)
Code: modeling/ridge/ridge.py

The model uses the eight original predictors, their squared terms, and pairwise
interactions (44 terms). The same five grouped validation folds from
concrete_split.csv are used for each of the 20 ridge penalties. The test set is
not used to choose the penalty.

PANEL (a) - which ridge penalty predicts best
- x-axis: ridge penalty lambda, shown on a logarithmic scale from 0.0001 to 10,000
- y-axis: mean validation RMSE across the five grouped folds, in MPa; lower is better
- Blue dots and line: average validation RMSE for each tested penalty.
- Light blue band: +/- one standard deviation across the five folds.
- Black diamond and vertical dashed line: selected penalty, lambda = 11.29.
  This is the setting with the lowest mean validation RMSE (8.42 MPa).

What to look for: penalties close to zero give similar errors. Moderate
regularization gives a small improvement, while very large penalties increase
RMSE because they restrict the model too much. The minimum is not sharply
separated from nearby settings, so the exact penalty should not be over interpreted.

PANEL (b) - how consistent the selected model is across folds
- x-axis: validation fold (1 through 5).
- y-axis: validation RMSE, in MPa.
- Each blue bar: RMSE on one held-out grouped fold using the selected penalty.
- Black horizontal line: mean validation RMSE (8.42 MPa).

What to look for: performance differs slightly between the folds. Fold 5 has
the highest error, showing that some held-out mixtures are harder to predict.
The five-fold standard deviation is about 0.55 MPa.

CAUTIONS
- Scaling is calculated from the training portion of each fold, after
  polynomial expansion, and is applied unchanged to the corresponding
  validation fold. This avoids using validation data during fitting.
- Ridge reduces the size of coefficients but does not guarantee that a
  polynomial relationship describes concrete strength correctly.
